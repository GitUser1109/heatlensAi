Why does Impervious Surface dominate model importance?

Impervious materials (asphalt, concrete) possess low thermal inertia and high volumetric heat capacity. They absorb shortwave radiation during peak daylight and reradiate longwave heat well into nocturnal hours, completely sealing natural soil and preventing evaporative cooling.

Correlation vs. Causation Caution

Permutation importance measures how dependent the Random Forest Regressor is on a given input feature. It is an algorithmic diagnostic—not mathematical proof of direct physical causality. In real urban ecosystems, vegetation coverage and impervious surfaces are strongly inversely correlated.

ENVIRONMENTAL GLOSSARY

Physical and Remote Sensing Definitions

NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index)

Formula: (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red)

A radiometric indicator of live green biomass calculated from satellite reflectance bands. Healthy chlorophyll absorbs red light and strongly reflects near-infrared (NIR). Values close to 1.0 denote dense canopy.

Impervious Surface Coverage (%)

Range: 0% to 100%

Artificial surfaces (roads, paved parking lots, rooftops) impenetrable to water. Prevents rainwater infiltration, reduces soil latent heat flux, and maximizes heat storage.

Tree Canopy Cover (%)

Range: 0% to 100%

The ground area covered by tree crowns when viewed from above. Provides direct solar radiation attenuation (shade) and pulls deep soil moisture into the boundary layer through transpiration.

Surface Albedo (Reflectance)

Range: 0.0 to 1.0

The fraction of solar irradiance reflected by the surface. Standard dark asphalt has a low albedo (~0.10), whereas cool reflective coatings reach 0.50–0.70, mitigating heat buildup.

Air Temperature vs. Land Surface Temperature (LST)

Crucial Scientific Distinction

Air temperature measures the thermal kinetic energy of the atmosphere 2 meters above ground. LST is the radiometric skin temperature of the ground surface. On sunny days, urban pavement LST can exceed air temperature by 15°C to 20°C!

SCIENTIFIC KNOWLEDGE BASE

Urban Heat Island Physics & Environmental Intelligence

Surface Radiative Equilibrium & Sensible Heat Flux

The urban surface energy balance is governed by: Rn + Qf = H + LE + G, where Rn is net all-wave radiation, Qf is anthropogenic heat emission (traffic, air-conditioning condensers, industrial equipment), H is turbulent sensible heat flux to the boundary layer, LE is latent heat flux (evaporative cooling), and G is conduction storage into building structures and asphalt pavement.

In heavily sealed commercial and industrial zones, low moisture availability suppresses LE to near zero. Consequently, absorbed solar radiation is partitioned almost entirely into sensible heating (H) and daytime thermal storage (G). At night, this stored heat is slowly released back to the lower atmosphere, generating the characteristic nocturnal Urban Heat Island (UHI).

Key Reference: Oke, T. R. (1982). The energetic basis of the urban heat island. Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society, 108(455), 1–24.

Radiometric Land Surface Temperature (LST) vs. 2m Ambient Air Temperature

A frequent source of public confusion is the difference between skin LST and ambient weather temperature:

  • Land Surface Temperature (LST): The radiometric radiative temperature emitted by surface facets (roofs, roads, tree crowns) measured by thermal infrared satellite sensors (e.g., Landsat Band 10). On a 34°C day in Mumbai, asphalt surface LST frequently reaches 52°C–58°C.
  • 2-Metre Air Temperature (T2M): The thermodynamic kinetic temperature of air molecules measured inside an aspirated Stevenson screen 2 metres above ground (e.g., NASA POWER reanalysis, IMD stations). Air temperature exhibits dampened spatial variance compared to the sharp micro-scale heterogeneity of LST.
Key Reference: Voogt, J. A., & Oke, T. R. (2003). Thermal remote sensing of urban climates. Remote Sensing of Environment, 86(3), 370–384.

USGS Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 Surface Temperature (ST) Pipeline

USGS computes Level-2 ST from Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS) Band 10 (10.60–11.19 µm) using a single-channel atmospheric correction algorithm: 

ST (Kelvin) = Digital_Number × 0.00341802 + 149.0
ST (°Celsius) = ST (Kelvin) − 273.15

Scientific Screening Requirements: Any automated ingestion must screen pixels using QA_PIXEL for clouds (bit 3), cloud shadow (bit 4), and cirrus (bit 2). Pixels with atmospheric transmission uncertainty > 2.5K in the ST_QA band must be discarded.

Official Guide: USGS Landsat Collection 2 Surface Temperature Technical Document

Copernicus Sentinel-2 Level-2A NDVI & Limitation of Vegetation Indices

Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is computed from 10m Bottom-Of-Atmosphere (BOA) surface reflectance: 

NDVI = (Band 8_NIR − Band 4_Red) / (Band 8_NIR + Band 4_Red)
where Band 8 is 842 nm (10m) and Band 4 is 665 nm (10m)

Scientific Caution: NDVI measures two-dimensional photosynthetically active biomass. It does not directly equate to volumetric tree canopy volume or shade. A manicured grass lawn can yield an NDVI of 0.65 while casting minimal solar shade on pedestrian sidewalks.

Urban Tree Canopy: Stomatal Evapotranspiration & Solar Interception

Mature urban shade trees cool cities through two coupled physical mechanisms:

  • Solar Radiation Attenuation (Shading): Tree canopies intercept 60%–90% of incident shortwave solar radiation, preventing underlying pavement and building walls from heating up during peak solar hours.
  • Latent Heat Transpiration: Trees extract deep moisture from the soil and vaporize it into the atmosphere through stomatal openings. A single mature neem or banyan tree can transpire over 200–400 litres of water daily, dissipating up to 1,000 MJ of heat energy!
Key Reference: Bowler, D. E., et al. (2010). Urban greening to cool towns and cities: A systematic review of the empirical evidence. Landscape and Urban Planning, 97(3), 147–155.

High-Albedo Reflective Surfaces (Cool Roofs & Cool Pavements)

Standard dark roofing materials absorb 85%–90% of solar radiation. Cool roofs coated with elastomeric acrylic, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), or white mineral tiles achieve solar reflectance (albedo) of 0.65–0.80 and thermal emittance > 0.85. This reduces surface temperatures by 15°C–25°C and cuts cooling electricity loads by 12%–28%.

Engineering Consideration: In dense urban street canyons, ground-level cool pavements can reflect solar radiation into adjacent glazed building facades and onto pedestrians, increasing mean radiant temperature (MRT) despite lower pavement surface temperatures.

Key Reference: Akbari, H., Pomerantz, M., & Taha, H. (2001). Cool surfaces and shade trees to reduce energy use and improve air quality in urban areas. Solar Energy, 70(3), 295–310.

Thermal Inequity & Climate Vulnerability in Mumbai

Urban heat exposure is deeply stratified across socioeconomic gradients. In informal settlements and high-density tenements (such as Dharavi, Govandi, and Kurla):

  • Corrugated Metal Roofs: Tin and asbestos roofing sheets absorb intense solar radiation and conduct extreme temperatures directly into uninsulated single-room dwellings.
  • Lack of Vegetative Canopy: Densely built wards have tree canopy coverage under 3%–5%, compared to 25%–35% in planned low-density residential enclaves (e.g., Malabar Hill, Cuffe Parade).
  • Inadequate Ventilation: Narrow alleys restrict convective wind penetration, causing stagnant nocturnal thermal entrapment.
Key Reference: Harlan, S. L., et al. (2006). Neighborhood microclimates and vulnerability to heat stress. Social Science & Medicine, 63(11), 2847–2863.
